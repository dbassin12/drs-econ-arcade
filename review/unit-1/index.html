How the curve works

Everyone gets the same 7 points. Here is why, and what it does.

  • 7 points is about 2 questions. Each question was worth 100 ÷ 28 = 3.57 points, so the curve gives back about 2 questions to everyone.
  • Nobody goes over 100. The top score was 93, and 93 + 7 = 100 exactly.
  • Your place in the class doesn't change. Everyone moves up the same amount, so the order stays the same. That's what makes a flat curve fair.
  • The spread doesn't change either. Standard deviation is about how far a typical score is from the average. Ours is 15.3 points. Every score and the average went up the same 7 points, so each score is still the same distance from the average. On the number line, the shaded band slides right but doesn't get wider.
  • Why curve at all? This was our first test written at AP difficulty, where the wrong answers are built to look right. The real AP exam doesn't score like a classroom test either. You'll see that in the calculator next.

What would this be on the AP exam?

The AP exam is scored on a 1 to 5 scale, and you don't need 90% of the points for a 5.

  • Part 1: Multiple choice60 questions in 70 minutes. Two thirds of your score.
  • Part 2: Free response3 questions in 60 minutes, including 10 minutes to read and plan. One third of your score. Question 1 is the long one. Questions 2 and 3 are short.
  • How the calculator adds it upEach multiple-choice question is 1 point. Each free-response point counts 1.5, which makes free response one third of the total. The total is 90.

Fix 1: Slide or shift?

Question 18: 12 of us missed it. Questions 15 and 22: 7 each.

Did the good's own price change? Slide along the curve. Did anything else change? Shift the whole curve.

Fix 2: Reading a PPC table

Question 7: 9 of us missed it. Question 8: 8 of us.

A number in the table tells you where you are. The change between two rows tells you what you gave up.

Brightland: computers and cars (from the test)

Why does the PPC bow out?

Fill in every row and the costs go 2, 4, 7, 11, 16. Each computer costs more cars than the one before, which is increasing opportunity cost, so the PPC bows outward (question 8, answer B). If every computer cost the same number of cars, the PPC would be a straight line.

The reason: resources aren't equally good at everything. The first workers Brightland moves to computers are the ones who were worst at building cars, so it loses only a few cars. By the 5th computer it has to move its best car builders.

Fix 3: Comparative advantage

Question 14: 14 of us missed it, our hardest question. Question 13: 10 of us.

First: is it an output problem or an input problem?

Output: how much can you make?

Sam can make 12 tables or 24 chairs in a day.

Cost of 1 table = chairs ÷ tables = 24 ÷ 12 = 2 chairs
Cost of 1 chair = tables ÷ chairs = 12 ÷ 24 = 1/2 of a table

Give up, over gain. Making more with the same resources is an absolute advantage.

Input: how long does it take?

Avi takes 4 hours to build a table and 2 hours to build a chair.

Cost of 1 table = time for a table ÷ time for a chair = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 chairs
Cost of 1 chair = time for a chair ÷ time for a table = 2 ÷ 4 = 1/2 of a table

Taking fewer hours is an absolute advantage.

Either way, the lower opportunity cost wins comparative advantage.

Question 13 was an input problem: hours, not amounts. If you use "give up, over gain" on hours, you get the answer backwards. Check question 13 in the calculator below.

Before the next test

Three things to do this week.

  1. Find your misses. Go through your test and find every question you got wrong in the chart above. Next to each one, write which idea it was testing.
  2. Redo 7, 13, 14, and 18 without looking at the key. Use today's rules: slide or shift, subtract between rows, check if it's a time problem, and remember that trade changes what you can have.
  3. Practice. Play Open Outcry, our Unit 1 review game, and do the AP Classroom topic questions for 1.2, 1.3, and 1.5.

Teacher plan for a 38-minute class
  1. Hand back tests (2 min). Students need them for "Find your misses".
  2. Scores and the curve (4 min). Toggle before and after. Point out that the band slides but doesn't stretch.
  3. AP calculator (3 min). Click "Our class average", then "Just over half". Ask: what surprised you?
  4. By question (2 min). Show the red bars and the three ideas.
  5. Fix 1, slide or shift (7 min). Vote on question 18 first. Then run "Price rises" and the other change on both tabs.
  6. Fix 2, PPC tables (6 min). Vote on question 7 first. Then click through the 1st to 5th computer.
  7. Fix 3, comparative advantage (8 min). Vote on questions 13 and 14 first. Then the output and input cards, the calculator, and the produce or consume graph.
  8. Try it (6 min). Students write answers on paper, then reveal. If time runs short, use it as an exit ticket or homework.
